Why Do We Celebrate Halloween?

Ancient Origins

The Celtic Samhain Festival

Over 2,000 years ago, the Celts of Ireland, the UK, and northern France celebrated Samhain (pronounced sow-in) on October 31st. It marked the end of the harvest season and the beginning of the dark, cold winter—a time associated with human mortality.

The Spirit Veil

Thinning of the Boundary

Celts believed that on the night before the new year (November 1st), the veil between the worlds of the living and the dead became ultra-thin. Spirits of the deceased were believed to return to earth, causing mischief and damaging crops.

Christian Adaptation

All Hallows' Eve

In the 8th century, Pope Gregory III designated November 1st as "All Saints' Day" to honor martyrs and saints. The eve before became known as All Hallows' Eve, which eventually shortened into Halloween.

Spooky Traditions & Their Meaning

🎃 The Legend of Jack-o'-Lanterns

Carving pumpkins originates from an Irish myth about "Stingy Jack," a trickster who tricked the Devil. When he died, he was condemned to wander the Earth with only a carved turnip lit by a burning ember. Irish immigrants adapted the legend in America using native pumpkins!

🎭 Wearing Scary Costumes

During Samhain, villagers wore masks and animal pelts whenever they left their homes after dark. The goal was to blend in with spirits wandering the roads so the ghosts would mistake humans for fellow ghosts and leave them unharmed.

🐈 Black Cats & Witches

In medieval Europe, black cats were believed to be "familiars"—supernatural assistants to witches—or even witches themselves shapeshifting to hide in the dark. This superstition cemented black cats as an iconic Halloween symbol.

🍎 Bobbing for Apples

When the Romans conquered Celtic territory, they merged Samhain with their autumn festival honoring Pomona, the goddess of fruit and trees. Bobbing for apples originated as a fortune-telling game for young adults to predict who would marry next.

🔥 Bonfires & Protection

Druid priests built giant sacred bonfires on hilltops to offer sacrifices to Celtic deities and help guide friendly ancestral spirits home while keeping malevolent entities at bay.

🍿 Modern Horror & Haunted Houses

Today, people celebrate autumn and Halloween by hosting costume parties, visiting commercial haunted houses, watching horror film marathons, and drinking spiced apple cider.

Why Do We Trick-or-Treat?

The custom of going door-to-door for treats evolved across three distinct historical eras:

1. Souling (Middle Ages): On All Souls' Day (November 2nd), poor villagers and children visited wealthy homes offering prayers for dead ancestors in exchange for small sweet pastries known as "soul cakes."
2. Guising (Scotland & Ireland): By the 19th century, children dressed up in disguises ("guises") and visited neighbors. Rather than just asking for food, they had to earn it by performing songs, telling jokes, reading poetry, or acting out tricks.
3. Bribing Pranksters (1930s–1950s): In early 20th-century America, young people celebrated Halloween by pulling mischievous pranks (like unhinging gates or tipping over outhouses). Communities popularized handing out packaged candy as a harmless way to "bribe" kids and stop the tricks!

How the World Celebrates Autumn & Spirits

Mexico

Día de los Muertos

Celebrated from Nov 1–2, "Day of the Dead" is a joyful celebration honoring deceased ancestors with colorful marigold flowers, sugar skulls, and home altars (ofrendas) filled with favorite foods.

Ireland

Púca Festival

Ireland honors Halloween's authentic Celtic roots at the Púca Festival with traditional storytelling, torchlight processions, bonfires, and traditional foods like Barmbrack fruit bread.

Japan

Kawasaki Halloween Parade

In Japan, Halloween is celebrated not by trick-or-treating, but through massive, highly elaborate cosplay parades where thousands gather in stunning costumes.

Spooky Halloween Fun Facts

🍬 Candy Consumption

Over 600 million pounds of candy are purchased in the United States alone every Halloween season—with chocolate taking up over 75% of total sales!

🌕 Full Moon Rarity

A full moon on Halloween night is surprisingly rare! It only occurs roughly once every 18 to 19 years. The last Halloween full moon was in 2020.

🎃 World Record Pumpkin

The heaviest Jack-o'-Lantern pumpkin ever recorded weighed over 2,700 pounds (over 1,200 kg)—heavier than a compact car!
